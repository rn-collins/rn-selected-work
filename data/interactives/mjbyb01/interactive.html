The tireless judge

In a famous study of 1,112 parole rulings, favourable rulings fell from about 65 percent to near zero within each session and jumped back after a food break (Danziger et al., 2011). WELL ESTABLISHED that the pattern is in the data. SCIENTISTS STILL DISAGREE on why.

One alternative (Glöckner, 2016): granting a request took longer on average, about 7.37 minutes against 5.21. A judge who never tires, but holds a case that cannot finish before the break until the next session, could produce a similar drop. Try it below.

How to read it

The judge in this simulation never gets hungry or tired, and every request has the same chance of deserving a yes all session. With scheduling on, the dots still slope down: late in a session there is less time left, the longer, favourable cases are the ones that no longer fit, and the held case opens the next session. With scheduling off, the dots stay near the dashed line. Case lengths follow Glöckner's simulation settings, which match the averages in the study.

In Glöckner's own simulations the drop was somewhat smaller than in the original data, and he notes they do not rule out order or depletion effects. The original authors replied that their trend held when they controlled for legal representation. SCIENTISTS STILL DISAGREE
