How long was that?

Gamers stopped after 12, 35 or 58 minutes did not, on average, underestimate how long they had played. Those told in advance that they would be asked guessed longer (Tobin et al., 2010). Try a tiny version: do a simple task until the page stops you, then guess how long it lasted.

What this does and does not show

This is a demonstration, not a measurement. A one-person, two-minute game cannot test anything, and the study ran sessions of 12 to 58 minutes in gaming centres. It shows the question the researchers asked.

Untested: the popular claim that casinos hide clocks so visitors lose track of time. We searched and found no study that tests it.

Three studies from one lab agree: in 129 regular slot players, an absorbed state called dark flow, which includes losing track of time, went with enjoyment and with problem-gambling scores (Dixon et al., 2019); studies of 136 and 110 players from the same lab found the same link with problem-gambling scores (Dixon et al., 2018; Kruger et al., 2022).

Two small studies agree: 30 regular gamblers chose smaller, sooner rewards more often in a slot-machine venue than in a café (Wagner et al., 2022), and an off-track betting site did the same for 16 of 20 people with gambling problems (Dixon, Jacobs & Sanders, 2006); neither tested non-gamblers.

Untested: whether a phone alarm helps. The one tested time reminder, a pop-up every minute in an online game, made no difference to how long 62 students played (Lavoie & Main, 2019).

One field study: 116 gamers did not underestimate their sessions on average; advance warning made estimates longer (Tobin et al., 2010).
