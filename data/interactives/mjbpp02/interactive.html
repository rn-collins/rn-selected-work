Pass, keep testing or fail

In the United States, a package is "child resistant" when it passes a test with real children aged 42 to 51 months. They try in pairs: 5 minutes alone, one demonstration by a tester, then 5 more minutes. Children are tested 50 at a time, up to 200, and the count of openings is checked against a table in the regulation (16 CFR 1700.20, Table 1).

Pick how many children have been tested so far and how many opened the package. The page reads the verdict from the regulation's table. It is a way to read the rule, not a test result for any product.

At 100 children and more, the pass rows start above zero because a package can only reach that panel after a "continue" result with fewer children. A package also has to meet the pass marks in 16 CFR 1700.15: at least 85 percent of children unable to open it before the demonstration and 80 percent after, and at least 90 percent of 100 adults aged 50 to 70 able to open and reclose it.

What this shows, and what it does not

  • A passing package may still be opened by some children in the test. "Child resistant" slows most young children down; it is not "childproof".
  • The rule is what the regulation requires, not a study finding.
  • Homes are not test rooms. In Canadian emergency data, 97.3 percent of detergent-packet cases with a recorded place happened at home, and 86.5 percent of 904 cases were children aged 4 or under (Zutrauen et al., 2024).
