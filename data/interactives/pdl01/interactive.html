Warp the word

How far can a word bend before your brain stops reading it?

spacing
needed

Plain lettering. Letter spacing is more than half the distance from your gaze, so the letters stand clear.

Well established
Letters closer together than a critical spacing jumble into a mess. Away from the centre of gaze, that spacing is roughly half the distance from where you look (the Bouma law), and it limits reading speed (Pelli & Tillman, 2008).
One small study
The brain's visual word form area still responded to words made only of moving or bright dots, as they became visible, in 6 adults (Rauschecker et al., 2011). Odd shapes may cost less than tight packing.
Our own synthesis, untested
Museum records describe Wes Wilson's lettering as drawn free-hand and stretched to fill the space (V&A), after Viennese Secession lettering (SAAM). Packing letters together would raise crowding, so the posters plausibly read slowly but surely. No study has tested the posters.

How to read the meter: the slider bends the letters and narrows the gaps between them, as space-filling lettering does. The meter compares the centre-to-centre spacing of the letters with half the distance from your chosen gaze point to the word, the rough Bouma rule. When the spacing falls below it, the letters are likely to crowd. Try a plain word with your gaze far away too: crowding is about spacing and distance, whatever the letter shapes. This is a geometric illustration of a rule from the research, drawn in code; it does not measure your own vision. Nothing on this page flickers, and nothing you do here is stored or sent.
